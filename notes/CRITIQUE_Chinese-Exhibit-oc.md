# Editorial Critique

## *My Recollections of the Chinese Exhibit at the Nelson-Atkins, 1975*

---

## Overview

This is a genuinely moving personal essay with strong emotional resonance and a distinctive narrator's voice. The central metaphor—transformation and invisibility through the dry-brush technique—carries the piece beautifully. The night-shift reverie, the solitude with the Galloping Horse, and the closing invocation of memory are the essay's heart. What follows are targeted suggestions for sharpening structure, prose, and clarity.

---

## Strengths

1. **Voice**: The narrator's personality comes through clearly—curious, earnest, self-aware about his younger self's limitations. This voice sustains the piece.

2. **Central Metaphor**: The dry-brush technique ("the goal was invisibility") is an inspired framing device. It connects the mundane task to something almost philosophical. This deserves emphasis.

3. **The Night-Shift Reverie** (paragraphs 37–46): This is the emotional core of the essay. The contrast between the daytime crowds and the nocturnal solitude, the description of the Galloping Horse, the meditation on endurance—these paragraphs are the essay's best writing.

4. **Concrete Details**: The Rockhill Road apartment, the south-side gardening interview, the red carpet warning, the bronze bells on continuous loop—these details ground the memoir in specific, verifiable sensory experience.

5. **Historical Context**: The brief framing of the exhibit within Cold War diplomacy and the "diplomatic shift" adds necessary weight without becoming didactic.

---

## Issues

### 1. Structure: The Historical Context Block (Paragraph 13)

Paragraph 13 is doing important work—establishing the geopolitical significance of the exhibit—but it breaks the narrative flow. As written, it feels like an encyclopedia entry inserted mid-story:

> "The much anticipated collection of Chinese cultural artifacts arrived in early April 1975, in the midst of the Cold War and at a moment when the country was still processing a historic diplomatic shift. Official US Foreign Policy towards China was changing..."

**Suggestion**: Weave this context into surrounding paragraphs rather than isolating it. Alternatively, expand it slightly and position it earlier, before the narrator discusses getting the job. The reader needs this context to understand why the exhibit mattered, but it should emerge naturally from the narrative rather than arriving as a stand-alone block.

### 2. Redundancy: The "Lines Around the Block"

Paragraph 47 mentions "the long lines that often stretched around the block," but this is the first mention of lines since the introduction (which only says the neighborhood would "adjust to the anticipated activity"). By paragraph 47, the reader has not yet been told about the lines.

**Suggestion**: Establish the crowds and lines earlier—perhaps when describing the job application (paragraph 15) or after getting hired (paragraph 27). The visitor-narration in paragraph 47 ("navigating the galleries full of patrons") would then feel like a return to a theme rather than its first introduction.

### 3. Disjunction: The Home Life Section (Paragraph 47)

Paragraph 47 shifts abruptly from the narrator's solitary night-shift experience to the apartment becoming "a kind of headquarters" for visiting friends and family. This section reads as an addendum and doesn't integrate with the essay's central meditation on solitude, visibility, and memory.

**Suggestion**: Either develop this section more fully (the social dimension of the exhibit is interesting and underexplored) or cut it and fold any essential point into the closing paragraphs. As written, it feels like a separate essay about hosting visitors that was stapled onto the main piece.

### 4. Disjunction: The Closing Paragraph (Paragraph 51)

Paragraph 51 begins with reflection ("What I could not have known was how enduring those graveyard hours would prove to be") and ends with a self-deprecating note ("I was still twenty-something, the pay was modest, and by five in the morning I was exhausted and mostly thinking about breakfast and sleep"). This tonal pivot—from elevated reflection to mundane fatigue—undercuts the closing's resonance.

**Suggestion**: The fatigue note could work if it were reframed. The contrast between historical significance and personal triviality is real and worth exploring, but the current phrasing ("mostly thinking about breakfast and sleep") reads as bathos rather than earned irony. Consider:

> "...but I also remember, with equal clarity, the exhaustion of a five a.m. morning, the weight of the paint container in my hand, the single thought of breakfast. Memory, it seems, holds both the momentous and the mundane without preference."

### 5. The Galloping Horse Name

Paragraph 43 calls it "the Galloping Horse" and then immediately introduces "the Flying Horse of Gansu." Paragraph 45 uses "the Galloping Horse." Paragraph 51 uses "the Flying Horse of Gansu."

**Suggestion**: Pick one name and use it consistently, or signal the alternative name once and then stick with your preferred term. The current oscillation is a minor distraction.

### 6. "Outlasting Dynasties and Oceans" (Paragraph 39)

This phrase is strong, but the sentence leading to it ("A bronze vessel cast thousands of years before did not require my admiration to be remarkable") needs a comma after "before."

### 7. "Touch-up painting started immediately when we arrived each night" (Paragraph 29)

This sentence is correct but slightly stiff. Consider: "Touch-up painting began the moment we arrived each night..."

### 8. "The Exhibit was a vast collection of ancient artifacts, timeless treasures" (Paragraph 43)

Remove the comma: "The Exhibit was a vast collection of ancient artifacts and timeless treasures."

### 9. "The phenomenon of the Exhibition found its way into our home life" (Paragraph 47)

"Found its way into" is vague and slightly off-tone for an essay that otherwise avoids cliché. Consider: "The Exhibition changed our home life too."

## Pacing

The essay has a natural rhythm, but the middle section (paragraphs 27–31) moves quickly through logistics—the job details, the shift, the carpet warning, the gallery transformation. This is information-dense and slightly breathless. The night-shift reverie (paragraphs 37–46) is where the essay slows down and breathes. Consider whether some of the logistical details could be trimmed or moved earlier, so the pacing reflects the essay's emotional architecture: anticipation → immersion → reflection.

---

## The Ending

The final image ("I can still hear the bronze bells echoing through the galleries and see the lithe, powerful figure of the Flying Horse of Gansu") is the right closing note. It returns to the two central images of the essay—the bells and the horse—and leaves the reader with something sensory and specific.

The penultimate sentence ("I am forever grateful for the memories the Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art gave me that spring of 1975") is sincere but generic. "Forever grateful" borders on cliché, and "the memories...gave me" is passive. Consider:

> "...and I remain, more than fifty years later, in their debt."

Or simply end with the sensory image and cut the gratitude statement entirely. The bells and the horse are gratitude enough.

---

## Summary of Recommendations

1. **Integrate paragraph 13** (Cold War context) into surrounding narrative.
2. **Introduce the crowds and lines earlier** to create dramatic contrast.
3. **Decide what to do with paragraph 47** (home life / hosting visitors)—develop or cut.
4. **Reframe the closing fatigue note** to preserve tonal coherence.
5. **Unify the naming** of the Galloping Horse.
6. **Address the pacing** in the logistical middle section.
7. **Trim or revise the final gratitude statement**; consider ending on the bells and horse.

---

## Final Assessment

This is a memoir with real emotional substance and a voice worth hearing. The strongest material—the interview, the dry-brush technique, the nocturnal solitude, the Galloping Horse, the bronze bells—should be amplified. The structural and prose issues are mostly fixable in revision. The essay's heart is beating; it just needs a little dry-brush work.
